How this is calculated
  1. Billable hours = (52 − vacation weeks) × hours per week × billable %
  2. Pre-tax income = desired income ÷ (1 − tax rate)
  3. Annual revenue target = pre-tax income + (monthly expenses × 12)
  4. Minimum hourly rate = annual revenue target ÷ billable hours
  5. Recommended rate = minimum × 1.15 (a 15% buffer for slow months, late payers and unexpected costs)

Tax is applied as a gross-up: you must earn more than your target income so that what is left after tax equals it. Expenses are added on top because they are not income. All figures are estimates — check local tax rules before pricing.

How to Calculate Your Freelance Hourly Rate

The calculator above follows the same six steps you can work through by hand:

1. Start with your income goal

Decide the annual income you want to be left with after tax — this is what you type into "Desired annual income".

2. Account for taxes

To keep that amount after tax, you need to invoice more than it. The calculator grosses it up by dividing by (1 − your estimated tax rate).

3. Add annual business expenses

Software, insurance, equipment and other costs come out of what you invoice, so twelve months of expenses are added on top of the grossed-up income.

4. Work out your real billable hours

Not every working hour is billable. Multiply your working weeks (52 minus vacation) by hours per week, then by the percentage of that time you actually bill to clients.

5. Divide revenue by billable hours

Divide the annual revenue target (income + expenses, grossed up for tax) by your billable hours. That gives the minimum hourly rate — the break-even point.

6. Add a buffer for the recommended rate

This calculator's recommended rate adds a 15% buffer on top of the minimum, to allow for quiet months, late-paying clients and unplanned costs. That 15% is a setting of this particular tool, not an industry standard or a universal rule — you're free to use a smaller or larger buffer, or none at all, depending on your own situation.

Why 2,080 Hours Is Not Your Real Billable Time

40 hours a week × 52 weeks = 2,080 hours. This is simply the result of multiplying a 40-hour work week by 52 weeks. It does not account for vacation, holidays, administration, sales, marketing, meetings or other non-billable work.

In practice, a working week also has to make room for things a client is not paying for directly: administration, sales, marketing, client communication, proposals, meetings, vacation, and other non-billable work. Once those are subtracted, the hours left to bill are fewer than 2,080 — and how much fewer depends entirely on you.

That's what the "Billable time" field in the calculator above is for: rather than assuming a fixed percentage, it lets you enter the share of your working hours you realistically expect to bill, based on your own time.

Example

Someone wants to keep $60,000 a year. They spend $500 a month on business costs, take 4 weeks off, work 40 hours a week, bill about 70% of that time, and estimate a 25% tax rate. Those are the values the calculator loads by default, so you can follow along above.

Step by step
Billable hours(52 − 4) × 40 × 70% = 1,344 h
Income after gross-up$60,000 ÷ (1 − 0.25) = $80,000
Annual revenue target$80,000 + ($500 × 12) = $86,000
Minimum hourly rate$86,000 ÷ 1,344 ≈ $63.99
Recommended hourly rate$63.99 × 1.15 ≈ $73.59
Daily rate (8 h)$73.59 × 8 ≈ $589
Monthly revenue target$86,000 ÷ 12 ≈ $7,167

Dividing $60,000 by 2,080 would have suggested about $29/h — far below the $73.59/h this person actually needs to invoice to end up with the income they wanted, once tax, expenses and non-billable time are accounted for. That is only a simple salary-to-hours comparison; it does not account for taxes, business expenses, vacation or non-billable work.

Frequently asked questions

What is a freelance hourly rate?

It's the amount a freelancer charges for each hour of billable work.

How do I calculate my freelance hourly rate?

You can calculate it by dividing your annual revenue target by the hours you can actually bill, taking taxes, business expenses and non-billable time into account — which is exactly what the calculator above does.

How many hours should a freelancer bill?

It depends on your line of work, your clients, and how much non-billable time your particular business needs. There's no universal number — track your own time and use the figure that reflects your situation.

Should I include taxes when calculating my freelance rate?

Yes. You need to generate enough revenue to cover your taxes and still reach your desired income after taxes are paid. Tax obligations depend on your country and personal situation, so check what applies to you.

Should business expenses be included in my freelance rate?

Yes. Software, equipment, insurance and other costs reduce the money you have left over as income, so they need to be factored into your rate.

Is the recommended rate the same for every freelancer?

No. It depends on your desired income, expenses, taxes, vacation time, working hours and billable percentage. As before, the 15% buffer this calculator uses is a setting of this particular tool, not a universal rule.

Can I use this calculator for consulting or contract work?

Yes, as long as that work is billed through an hourly rate. Some work may be better suited to project-based pricing or retainers instead.
