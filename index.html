How this is calculated
  1. Billable hours = (52 − vacation weeks) × hours per week × billable %
  2. Pre-tax income = desired income ÷ (1 − tax rate)
  3. Annual revenue target = pre-tax income + (monthly expenses × 12)
  4. Minimum hourly rate = annual revenue target ÷ billable hours
  5. Recommended rate = minimum × 1.15 (a 15% buffer for slow months, late payers and unexpected costs)

Tax is applied as a gross-up: you must earn more than your target income so that what is left after tax equals it. Expenses are added on top because they are not income. All figures are estimates — check local tax rules before pricing.

How it works

The calculator turns an income goal into an hourly rate by working backwards through the four things that sit between what a client pays you and what you keep.

Billable hours

The hours a client actually pays for. Your working week also contains admin, invoicing, proposals, marketing and learning. Only the billable slice earns money, so that is what the rate is divided by.

Taxes

Your income goal is what you want to keep. To be left with it after tax, you have to invoice more. The calculator divides by (1 − tax rate) rather than adding the percentage on top, which is the mathematically correct way to gross up.

Business expenses

Software, hardware, insurance, an accountant, a coworking desk. These are paid out of what you invoice, so twelve months of expenses are added on top of your income target.

Minimum hourly rate

The break-even point: the annual revenue target divided by your billable hours. Charge below this and, over a full year, you fall short of your goal.

Recommended hourly rate

The minimum plus a 15% buffer for quiet months, late payments and costs you didn't plan for. It is a starting point for your own pricing decision, not a number that is right for everyone.

Why dividing your salary by 2,080 doesn't work

2,080 is 40 hours × 52 weeks: the hours of a full-time employee who never takes a holiday. Using it as a freelancer produces a rate that is far too low, for four reasons.

  • You take time off. Nobody bills 52 weeks. Subtract your holidays, and public holidays if you take them.
  • Not every working hour is billable. The time you spend finding clients, writing proposals, chasing invoices and keeping your skills current is real work that no client pays for directly.
  • An employer's cost is bigger than a salary. Payroll taxes, equipment, software, insurance and paid leave are all carried by your employer. As a freelancer you carry them yourself.
  • Tax comes out of the invoice, not the salary. A gross salary figure and a freelance invoice are not comparable numbers.

The effect is not small. Once holidays and non-billable time are removed, the hours you can actually sell are typically a good deal fewer than 2,080 — and every hour removed pushes the required rate up.

A worked example

Someone wants to keep $60,000 a year. They spend $500 a month on business costs, take 4 weeks off, work 40 hours a week, bill about 70% of that time, and estimate a 25% tax rate. Those are the values the calculator loads by default, so you can follow along above.

Step by step
Working weeks52 − 4 = 48
Billable hours48 × 40 × 70% = 1,344 h
Pre-tax income needed$60,000 ÷ (1 − 0.25) = $80,000
Annual expenses$500 × 12 = $6,000
Annual revenue target$80,000 + $6,000 = $86,000
Minimum hourly rate$86,000 ÷ 1,344 h ≈ $63.99/h
Recommended hourly rate$63.99 × 1.15 ≈ $73.58/h

Dividing $60,000 by 2,080 would have suggested about $29/h — less than half of what this person needs to invoice to end up with the income they wanted.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my freelance hourly rate?

Start from the income you want to keep, gross it up for tax, add a year of business expenses, then divide by the hours you can realistically bill — not by every hour you work. That is exactly what the calculator above does, and it shows each step so you can check the arithmetic.

How much should a freelancer charge per hour?

There is no single correct figure. It depends on your costs, your tax situation, how much time you can bill, your experience and what your market will pay. The calculator gives you the floor your own numbers imply; where you price above that floor is a commercial decision, and comparing against rates in your field and country is worth doing before you commit.

How many billable hours should a freelancer work?

Most freelancers find that a meaningful share of the week goes to non-billable work, so billing every scheduled hour is unrealistic. Rather than aiming for a target percentage, track your own time for a few weeks and use the figure you actually observe — an honest number here matters more to the result than any other input.

Should freelancers charge for non-billable hours?

Not as line items on an invoice, usually — clients pay for outcomes, not your admin. Instead, non-billable time is priced into the hourly rate, which is why it appears in this calculation. If it isn't in your rate, you are absorbing it for free.

Is this rate before or after tax?

The rates shown are what you invoice, before tax. The income figure you enter is what you want to be left with afterwards.

Does it work in my currency?

The maths is currency-agnostic — only the formatting shows dollars. Enter your own figures and read the results in your own currency.
